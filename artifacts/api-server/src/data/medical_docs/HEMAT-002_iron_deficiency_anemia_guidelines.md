## Overview & Diagnostic Criteria
Iron deficiency anemia occurs when body iron stores are insufficient to support normal red blood cell production. It is diagnosed when hemoglobin falls below reference ranges (Adult Males <13.5 g/dL, Adult Females <12.0 g/dL) in combination with low serum ferritin (<30 ng/mL) and decreased transferrin saturation (<20%).

## Common Causes
The primary causes of iron deficiency anemia include:
1. Chronic blood loss (heavy menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding from ulcers or gastritis, frequent blood donation).
2. Inadequate dietary iron intake (insufficient consumption of iron-rich foods such as red meat, poultry, legumes, and fortified grains).
3. Malabsorption syndromes (celiac disease, Crohn's disease, post-gastric bypass surgery impairing duodenal iron absorption).
4. Increased iron demand (pregnancy, lactation, rapid growth spurts in adolescence).

## Clinical Symptoms & Warning Signs
Common warning signs and clinical symptoms of iron deficiency anemia include:
- Persistent unexplained fatigue, generalized weakness, and lethargy.
- Pale skin, mucosal pallor, or pale inner lower eyelids (conjunctival pallor).
- Shortness of breath (dyspnea) or heart palpitations during mild physical exertion.
- Dizziness, lightheadedness, or persistent headache.
- Cold hands and feet due to impaired thermal regulation.
- Brittle, spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia) or sore, smooth tongue (glossitis).
- Unusual cravings for non-nutritive substances such as ice, dirt, or starch (pica).

## Management & Evaluation Rules
Patients presenting with confirmed iron deficiency anemia require clinical evaluation to identify the underlying source of iron loss or malabsorption. Oral iron supplementation (ferrous sulfate/gluconate with Vitamin C to enhance absorption) is standard initial therapy, followed by repeat hemoglobin and ferritin testing at 4 to 8 weeks.
